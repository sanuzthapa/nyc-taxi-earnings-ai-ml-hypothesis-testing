Do payment methods change NYC taxi fares? An A/B test, a model, and a calculator

An end-to-end walk through ingestion from the NYC OpenData SoQL API, cleaning, a card-vs-cash hypothesis test, and fare prediction with Linear Regression and Random Forest.

1 · The question

Does the way a passenger pays (credit card or cash) relate to the metered base fare? And can we predict that fare from information known before the trip starts?

The pipeline has four stages, one notebook each: 01_soql_ingestion → 02_eda_cleaning → 03_hypothesis_testing → 04_model_training, with reusable code in src/.

2 · Getting the data

Trips come from the Socrata endpoint 4b4i-vvec. Server-side SoQL picks the 17 columns we need and pages through the results:

$select = vendorid, tpep_pickup_datetime, …, total_amount
$limit  = 50000
$order  = :id
Sampling caveat. Ordering by :id returns the first 50,000 rows, almost all from 1 January 2023, a Sunday. Everything below describes that day, not the whole year. Weekday rush hour barely appears (1 trip in the original run).

4 · The A/B test

Hypotheses: H₀: μcard = μcash; HA: they differ. Welch's t-test, α = 0.05.

Mean base fare, original run (card n = 33,948, cash n = 10,728)
Card $19.76
Cash $19.24

t = 2.92, p = 0.0035. The 52¢ gap is statistically significant but small (about 3 % of the mean; bars start at $0).

Reading this honestly: fare_amount excludes tips, so the gap is not tips. It is also an observational comparison, because passengers choose how to pay. A significant p-value does not show that nudging people toward card would raise fares. Notebook 03 adds an effect size, a confidence interval and a Mann–Whitney check to keep the result in proportion.

5 · Predicting the fare

Distance and duration are unknown before a ride, so the model uses route history: the average distance and duration of past trips between the same pickup and drop-off zones.

A leakage trap

The first version computed these route averages over all rows, including the test set. For a route seen once, “mean distance” was that trip's own distance, which is almost the answer. The repo now fits the route table on the training split only (RoutePairAggregator) and falls back to the global mean for unseen routes. Notebook 04 quantifies how much the old approach flattered the scores.

Mean distance dominated (Random Forest importance ≈ 95 %), then mean duration (≈ 4 %). Passenger count, vendor and rush hour were negligible.

The TLC reference uses the published standard rate: $3.00 initial charge, then $0.70 per ⅕ mile above 12 mph or $0.70 per minute at or below 12 mph. The real meter switches continuously and this tool applies one rate to the whole trip. It excludes surcharges, tolls and tips, and is a sanity check, not a quote.

7 · Limitations

  • One-day sample unless you re-sample with a date-spread where clause.
  • Observational data: the A/B test shows association, not cause.
  • Route averages need history; unseen routes fall back to a global mean.
  • Base fare is mostly a deterministic meter formula, so high R² is expected and says little about business value.
